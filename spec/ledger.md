# Working ledger: points to revisit while writing UNAUTHZ.md

Transitional file beside the draft. Each entry names the section it belongs to, the point, and what resolving it looks like. Entries leave this file when the section absorbs them.

## Order of work

1. Solve the model of who belongs to what and what is acted on, as relations, and show it rebuilds every platform in `reference-platforms.md`. The place key is a result computed from those relations, not the starting structure.
2. Only then decide how permissions and place keys map to each other, in storage and in carriers.

Working model under test for step 1, stated by the author on 2026-11-05, not yet settled:

- An organisation is itself a group, and groups nest; a segment under another segment says "this group, or this container, belongs to that one". The levels are the application's: organisation and team, owner and repository, or a single tenant.
- The resource acted on is named by the permission asked (`media:read`), not by the place. The place says under whose control the thing sits: the nesting of groups, continued where needed by the container path of the thing (a folder path under the group that controls it).
- At question time the application builds the place key from that hierarchy, typically from the route, and looks it up in what the session carries. Nothing else is needed at the endpoint.
- Open case: a group granted something at a place that does not sit beneath it (a GitHub team on a repository of the organisation, a directory group bound at a Google Cloud project, a Drive folder shared with a group).
- Hypothesis for that case, to be tested, not settled. Prior art: an LDAP group is an entry of the tree named by its path and lists its members; a Zanzibar grant may name `group#member` as its subject; Cedar tests `principal in Group` and `resource in Folder` as two independent conditions; an Azure role assignment is principal (user or group), role, scope. Extension proposed: the subject of a statement may be a group, written as a reserved subject made of `@` followed by the place key of the group. Such a statement applies to a subject at a target when two independent conditions hold: the subject is a member of that group, a fact the application supplies as pairs (subject, place key); and the place where the statement sits covers the target. Nothing else changes: the statement keeps its three parts; roles, exclusions and personal denies work as before; group statements only add; the store still looks statements up by subject and exact key, a group being one more subject; grants stay keyed by place because group statements are folded into a member's grants when they are issued; flat RBAC uses no group. Decided: the engine infers nothing about memberships; an application that wants a member of a group to count as a member of the groups above it expands the list itself when grants are issued, and one that prefers to fail closed does not. To decide with it: that issued grants go stale when a membership changes and the application must reissue; that changing a group's members is a way of granting which the model does not see; that recovery never depends on a group.

Findings from running the working model against WordPress, Kubernetes, Google Drive and GitHub on 2026-11-06. All four were reproduced as documented; the group hypothesis covered the team on a repository, the folder shared with a group and the cluster-wide binding to a group. Points the run raised:

- Grants carry no expiry: a statement that expires after grants were issued stays in the carrier. Either an entry carries the earliest expiry of what it holds, or grants live no longer than the soonest expiry among the statements they came from.
- A wide allow with places closed inside it is expressed with a permission per state: the application asks a different permission for a thing in the closed state, and the wide statement never gave that one. Drive's limited-access folder, WordPress's private posts and Discord's private channels all reproduce this way. It is an application pattern, not a construct of the model.
- A Discord role that takes a permission away from its members is not expressible: statements about a group only add. Discord is in the boundary set for that algorithm.
- Directory groups (Kubernetes, Google Cloud, Azure) are not places on their platforms; naming a group by a place forces a level that exists only to name groups. It works; decide whether that artifact is acceptable.
- A call site sometimes needs all of several permissions at once (editing someone else's published post). The application combines them today.

Findings from running the same application on real asynchronous storage on 2026-11-06, once on document-style files and once on a relational store, with every call measured:

- A request answered from grants in a cookie-borne session called neither the statements store, nor the directory of memberships, nor the session store; only the resource was read. The constraint that an endpoint never calls the statements store holds.
- Whether a store can filter decides the bytes moved, not the model. The relational store returned the three chain entries of a server-side session (123 of 2040 bytes) and the statements of one organisation at issuing (244 of 5803 bytes); the document store returned everything both times. The model's part is to keep one entry per place key so that a store can filter by exact key or by what lies under a place.
- Selecting everything under a place must not use pattern matching: canonical keys contain `%` from percent-encoding and may contain `_`, both wildcards in SQL `LIKE`. A half-open byte range from the key followed by `/` to the key followed by `0` is exact under binary collation. Section 9 should say so.
- Forty places carrying the same grant repeat it forty times: most of a 2040-byte carrier was one identical grant and a common key prefix. Evidence for the question in step 2 of the order of work, whether grants map places to permissions or permissions to places.
- A stored role that references a preset costs a document store a wasted read, since a store cannot know which names are presets. The contract for loading stored roles may need the names to skip.
- A check made against the store costs three round trips at least (memberships, statements on the chain, the resource), four when a stored role is involved.
- Staleness behaved as predicted: after a stored role was edited or a membership ended, issued grants kept the old answer, a check against the store gave the new one, and reissuing aligned them.

Targets set by the author on 2026-11-06, with the accounting that goes with them:

- What is counted is the round trips the check itself needs before it can answer: reads of the session store, of the statements store, of the directory of memberships. What the application reads to perform the action afterwards is application logic and is not counted. Round trips issued together are still several round trips; they only share one wait.
- A request answered from grants in a cookie-borne session costs no round trip at all. Round trips happen only when grants are issued or refreshed. A store may sit a continent away at about 300 ms a round trip, which is the reason such a session exists.
- A request answered from a server-side session costs exactly one round trip, the read of the session.
- The model does not control the application's storage. When the session and the content the action needs live in the same store, the application can fetch both in one query; the model's part is to provide what makes that possible, to the application and to the store alike: the list of keys to read for a target, and a reader that works on the entries returned, whoever fetched them. A document store reads one document at a time and cannot combine; that is its nature, not the model's concern.

Measured on real storage on 2026-11-06, with those definitions: a cookie-borne session cost no authorization round trip; a server-side session cost one, returning only the entries of the target's chain, and when sessions and content shared a relational store one query served both the check and the action; a check made against the statements store cost two round trips on a relational store (memberships, then the statements on the chain) and five on documents; issuing cost one round trip on a relational store holding memberships and statements together, through a single query returning the subject's groups, the statements about the subject and those groups, and the stored roles those statements name, and it issued the same grants as the separate reads in every case tried; on documents issuing cost seven.

Consequences for the draft: Section 8 should define the read of grants as "the entries at these keys" plus a reader over entries, independent of who fetched them; Section 9 should describe a single read for issuing that takes a subject and returns its groups, the statements about the subject and those groups, and the stored roles they name, so that a store holding all three answers in one round trip, and should state what is and is not counted as the model's round trips. The rule by which an application lets membership of a group count for the groups above it must be expressible inside that read, since applying it between two reads is what forced a second round trip.

Flat RBAC needs neither a place nor a membership; that much is settled. The content of Section 6 in the draft (key grammar, alternation, coverage by prefix) stands as a serialization only; what a place is, what the structure declares, and how groups relate to places are open until step 1 is done.

## Section 6, Place

- **A place says where, but nothing says what a place is about.** A level is a name and a position in the structure; the model does not relate it to the catalog. Nothing states which resources live at or under a level, so nothing says that `billing:read` is meaningless at a document, or that a statement at an organisation is about the repositories inside it. Existing systems all tie the two: Kubernetes resources are namespaced or cluster-scoped, Google Cloud permissions belong to resource types that sit at fixed levels of the hierarchy, Zanzibar relations are declared per object type. Study what the structure should declare about each level (the resources it contains, the permissions that are meaningful there, whether the level is itself a resource) before more of Section 6 is settled. This is an open problem, not a wording issue: membership, public levels and singleton levels all depend on its answer.

- **Appendix C grammar for keys.** Write the ABNF: `place = "/" / 1*segment`, `segment = "/" level "/" value`, level lowercase ASCII identifier, value `1*pchar` per RFC 3986 with the canonical percent-encoding rule, reserved values `*`, `.`, `..`, no trailing `/`, no empty segment. Keys must be valid RFC 3986 `path-absolute`.
- **Singleton levels.** With one unnamed root, a top that has exactly one instance (the platform side next to tenants) still needs a value, `/platform/main`. Decide whether the structure may declare a level as a singleton, printed with a fixed value so that keys stay parseable without the structure, or whether the dummy value is accepted as is.
- **Open structure and maximum depth.** Decide whether an application may declare an open structure (any level name, any nesting, grammar checking only) for prototypes, and how a maximum depth is declared for self-nesting levels. Both are declarations; the engine never assumes either.

## Section 5, Catalog

- **Open access is the application's.** A reserved subject for "anyone", a list of levels where it could be placed and a permission gating it were drafted and withdrawn on 2026-11-06. What is open to anyone is defined by the application in its own terms, without an engine or a session; where it wants coverage by place, it uses an ordinary subject of its own. The model reserves no subject and no permission name.

- **Structured resource names (idea, not proposed).** An application names the state of a thing on the resource (`private-post:edit`), and the model treats `post` and `private-post` as unrelated. Nothing then says "this action on every state of this one resource" in one entry, and a wildcard on the action reaches every state. Kubernetes writes a variant as `pods/log` under the same verbs. Allowing such a form would change what a permission is, so it needs a need that naming and exclusions cannot meet. Evidence for naming the state on the resource: every standard and engine surveyed ([XACML 3.0](https://docs.oasis-open.org/xacml/3.0/xacml-3.0-core-spec-os-en.html), NIST SP 800-162, [Cedar](https://docs.cedarpolicy.com/policies/policy-examples.html), SpiceDB, Open Policy Agent) carries it on the resource; the action carries it only on four cloud platforms, each inconsistent with itself, and mostly for operations that exist only in that state.

## Section 7, Decision

- **Membership and grants to a set of subjects (under study, nothing settled).** Need: say that a subject belongs to a place or to a group (an organisation, a team), and give every such subject a baseline with one statement instead of one per subject. Evidence gathered on 2026-11-05 from standards and platforms:
  - Every standard that defines an engine treats membership as input supplied to it by another authority: XACML ("roles are expressed as subject attributes", assignment "outside the scope of the PDP"), Cedar ("your application must gather all of the relevant information"), AuthZEN (group memberships as a subject property), NIST SP 800-162, and Kubernetes in practice (groups come from the authenticator). Directory standards store it on the group and leave authorization meaning out of scope: SCIM (RFC 7643 `Group.members`), LDAP (RFC 4519 `member`). Only NIST RBAC (user assignment) and Zanzibar (groups as usersets) store membership with the same relation as grants. No standard defines "member of an organisation" or "guest"; RFC 9068 borrows SCIM's `groups`, `roles`, `entitlements` as token claims.
  - Platforms: a guest is always "holds an inner grant, lacks the member record" (GitHub outside collaborator, Notion guest, Drive non-member), and the baseline never reaches guests. The baseline is a container setting (GitHub base permission), the member role itself (GitLab), or one grant to a set principal (Discord `@everyone`, Kubernetes `system:authenticated`, a domain). Membership inherits downward wherever containers nest. A ban is always a separate record from membership. Residue after removal is the most documented failure (GitLab SCIM leftovers, Azure "identity not found", Google `deleted:` bindings). Token-carried membership is stale and bounded in size (Entra 200 groups, Kubernetes unrevocable tokens).
  - Candidate shapes, none chosen: (a) membership as a fact distinct from statements, supplied to the engine with the question, with set subjects addressed by ordinary additive statements; (b) membership as holding a marker permission or role at the place, which reproduces GitLab and NIST core but leaves the baseline unsolved and separates guests only by convention; (c) membership derived from holding any grant, withdrawn: it is how every platform defines a guest; (d) application-defined set subjects only, the Kubernetes arrangement, of which (a) is the place-aware variant.
  - Decided on 2026-11-05: membership is a fact distinct from the catalog and from statements, supplied to the engine by the application, shape (a). Shape (b) is rejected: a permission that says what someone is, instead of what someone may do, is a category error and confuses the catalog.
  - Concern to design against: a place key already names the organisation, unit or project. If membership is carried under a second name (a group identifier) or carried together with grants that are keyed by the same places, the same information is stored and transmitted twice. Hypotheses to test against the reference platforms, none settled: (1) a group has no name of its own, it is "the members of a place", so membership is the pair (subject, place key) and one naming system serves organisations, units and teams, as an LDAP group is an entry in the directory tree named by its path and a Zanzibar group is `object#member`; a statement is then about one subject or about the members of a named place, and that place need not be the place where the statement sits; (2) a carrier holds either grants or membership, never both: membership is an input to issuing grants and to checks made against the store, and grants already folded for a subject need nothing else at the endpoint; (3) grants are issued for one base place, the organisation the session is acting in, with keys written relative to it, as identity products scope a token to one active organisation.
  - Whatever the shape, the model must state: whether sets are additive only; what happens to personal statements when membership ends; whether changing membership is an authority-checked act or outside the model, as in XACML; that recovery never depends on a set.

- **Which statement is reported as the reason.** When several statements decide the same way (two allows, or two denies, covering the target), the decision names one of them. The old draft said "the first in input order", which depends on the store's ordering and breaks determinism across stores. Define an order that depends only on the statements: for example the nearest place first, then rule before assignment, then a stable order on the permission and role name.
- **Guest sharing under a wider deny.** A document shared with an outside guest is an allow at `/org/org-1/doc/d42`. If the tenant also carries a deny of `doc:read` on that guest at `/org/org-1`, the deny wins: a deny is a ban and a share does not undo it. "Guests cannot read by default" is default-deny, not a deny rule, so a share alone works. Nearest-first precedence applies to nothing in this model: allows accumulate along the chain, denies veto everywhere. The worked example must show both cases.
- **Deny of the wildcard `*`.** Hypothesis: a rule denying `*` is legal and acts as a gate or ban at the place where it is placed, what it gates being application logic (a suspended account, a frozen tenant). Since deny is exact, a deny of `*` denies each permission exactly, so it beats every allow at any covering place, including an allow of `*`. Revalidate while writing the decision procedure: interaction with "deny is exact, never implied", with implications, with grants issuance (an empty entry or an explicit marker), and with authority (who may place a deny of `*`, given "hand out only what you hold" and "restrictions come from at least as wide a place").
- **Wildcards and implications.** A role `*:*` minus `billing:read` still yields `billing:manage`, and `billing:manage` implies `billing:read`, so the holder reads billing anyway. Consistent with "deny is exact, implications never deny" and with the author's choice of `*:*`, but the decision procedure must state it and the worked example should show it once.

## Section 8, Grants

- **Non-redundant grants: what is written in the draft and what remains.** Written: a grant holds only what its own place gives, allows and denies; reads walk the chain; wildcards carried as written; presets carried by name; stored roles carried as their resolved permissions. Remaining: the format itself (field names, how allows, denies and preset names sit in one entry). Decided by the author for the version string: it is made of components, one per thing whose change makes carried grants worth recomputing, namely the catalog, the structure, the engine together with its presets, and the minifier when one is used; each component changes only when its own input changes in a way that matters, so an application comparing the string learns that a recompute is due, wherever it keeps its sessions. Still to define: what exactly each component fingerprints, and which engine changes count.
- **Stored roles go stale in a carrier, and that is accepted.** A stored role is copied into grants as its resolved permissions, so an edit to the role is invisible to a carrier already issued; nothing in the carrier can reveal it. This is the known limit of any self-contained token and is accepted, as it is for tokens in general. What Section 8 should still say: the exposure lasts until the grants are next issued, so the lifetime of grants bounds it; an application that wants it shorter keeps a counter on its side, per subject or per stored role, stamps it into grants and compares it when it refreshes a session, which reads the application's own session data and never the statements. To verify against their texts before citing: the recommendation of short-lived tokens in the OAuth security best current practice, token revocation and introspection (RFC 7009, RFC 7662), and the OpenID Shared Signals and Continuous Access Evaluation work, which exists because of this limit.
- **Wildcards carried as written is on trial.** Chosen without evidence either way. Revisit when an implementation can measure carrier size and read cost for both forms, and confirm that a wildcard read against the catalog cannot answer differently from the statements it came from.
- **Prior art still to verify before citing in the draft.** Carrying compact facts and deriving at read: RFC 9068 (`groups`, `roles`, `entitlements` claims) and Kerberos group identifiers were named from memory; Biscuit tokens and RFC 9396 authorization details were not checked against their texts.
- **Issuing scope is the application's choice.** One application keeps every organisation's grants in one session; another reissues when the user switches organisation or project. The model must allow issuing for the whole tree or for one base place with keys relative to it.
- **Reading only what is asked.** A read needs the entries of the target's chain and nothing else, so a session store can return exactly those keys instead of the whole set. How a store does that is a storage concern; the model only guarantees that the chain's keys are sufficient.

- **Freshness with groups (depends on the group hypothesis in "Order of work").** Constraint from the author: answering a question at an endpoint must never call the statements store; the session, a cookie-borne token or a server-side record, is the only thing read. Group statements are therefore folded into a member's grants when they are issued. Two kinds of staleness follow and Section 8 must address both: a membership change stales one subject, whom the application knows and reissues; a change to a statement about a group stales every member at once, which needs either a short lifetime of grants or a counter per group that the application bumps and compares when it refreshes a session. Size grows with the number of places where a subject holds something directly or through groups, not with the number of members; issuing for one base place with relative keys and the minified form are the levers. A binding may instead keep each group's folded grants once in a shared cache and carry only personal grants and the group list; that is an optimization, not a requirement of the model.

- **Field names.** The old draft's `r` (reach key map) is retired with the word; candidate `g` for the per-place grants map, `v` version, `at` issued-at; decide together with the canonical format.

- **Minified form of grants.** The model defines one canonical form of grants: place keys and permissions written in full. An application MAY carry grants in a **minified** form: a substitution that replaces each word of the model with a shorter token, the way Morse code gives the most frequent letters the shortest signals. What Section 8 must define:
  - The substitution works word by word, never on the payload as a whole. The words are the level names of the structure, the resources and actions of the catalog, and the names of presets. Values of segments (`org-1`, `u7`) are application data the model does not know, so they stay as they are; the application may shorten its own ids on its side, outside the model.
  - The dictionary is derived deterministically from the catalog, the structure and the minifier version, and from nothing else, so it never travels with the grants and two parties with the same three inputs build the same dictionary. Frequency comes from the catalog itself: an action that appears under many resources (`read`, `manage`) earns a one-character token, a resource or level that appears once earns a longer one.
  - The substitution is injective: distinct words get distinct tokens, and a token is never a prefix of another in a position where it could be confused, so the minified form reads back without ambiguity. The lossless round-trip is the Section 4 invariant.
  - The version string of grants identifies the minifier and its version next to the catalog and structure fingerprints; a change in any of the three forces a reissue. Without a minifier the version carries no minifier part.
  - Because the substitution is word by word, a reader answers a question by minifying the chain keys of the target and the asked permission and matching tokens against the carried entries, with the same result as expanding every entry first. An engine still reads and writes canonical forms only; the minifier is a tool at the carrier boundary, library-provided or application-provided, never part of the engine.

## Section 10, Authority

- **A8 restricts by place of the deny, it should restrict by width of the author.** As written, a deny of P on X at a place is refused whenever X holds P from strictly wider, whoever places it. So an org manager who granted X a permission at `/org/org-1` cannot write "everywhere in the org except unit u7": nobody can. The intent was to stop a narrower manager from restricting a wider holder (the rescue path). Re-derive as: a deny of P on X may be authored only by a manager whose management covers the place where X holds P from; the deny itself may sit anywhere under that. The breached unit manager is still refused; the org manager is not. G3 is restated accordingly; G5 is unaffected because removal needs management at the deny's place or wider. Resource-state restrictions (published content, frozen projects) are not this case: they belong in the question the application asks, as a state-specific permission.

- **Editing a referenced role.** Adding a permission to `support` adds it to every role that references `support`, at every place where any of those roles is assigned. Decide who may edit an upstream role and at which place they must hold what they add. This is the escalation surface the old audit hit (a stored role that reached the root) and merges with the open question on stored-role ownership and where a stored role may be assigned.
- **Deleting or renaming a referenced role.** Refuse while references exist, or cascade; decide, and decide what the audit record shows.
- **Role name spaces.** A stored role MUST NOT reuse a preset's name: references are by name, so a duplicate could redirect a reference. State where uniqueness is enforced (definition, validation, both).
